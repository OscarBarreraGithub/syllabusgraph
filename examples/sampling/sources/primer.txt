Counting and random experiments

A fraction expresses a count relative to a total. In a random experiment, the outcome is not known before the experiment occurs. A sample space lists the outcomes considered possible. An event is a set of outcomes. For equally likely outcomes in a finite sample space, an event probability is the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes. The probability is between zero and one. A finite list of possible outcomes does not itself establish equal likelihood.

Teaching task: list the outcomes of two fair coin tosses, identify the event of exactly one head, and calculate its probability. Explain which assumptions make counting appropriate.

Turning outcomes into numbers

An indicator is a random variable that is one when an event happens and zero otherwise. It converts event questions into numerical questions. The expected value of a finite discrete random variable is the sum of its values weighted by their probabilities. The expected value of an event indicator equals the probability of the event. An expected value need not be one of the possible observed values.

Teaching task: compute the expected indicator for the event in the coin experiment, then compare that average with the possible values of the indicator.

Why samples disagree

A sample mean is the arithmetic average of observed numerical values. Repeating a sampling procedure produces different sample means: this is sampling variability. With independent, identically distributed observations of finite variance sigma squared, the variance of the sample mean is sigma squared divided by the number of observations. Its standard deviation is sigma divided by the square root of the sample size. This standard deviation is the standard error of the mean. Repeated observations from a dependent process need not obey this formula.

Teaching task: derive the variance of the average using independence, and predict the effect of multiplying the sample size by four.

Intervals describe a procedure

Under independent normal observations with known population standard deviation, the sample mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors gives an approximately 95 percent confidence procedure for the population mean. Across repeated samples, about 95 percent of the intervals produced by this procedure cover the fixed population mean. The formula needs its model assumptions. It does not guarantee coverage for arbitrary data or assign a frequentist probability to a fixed parameter after a particular interval is observed.

Teaching task: construct an interval in a stated model and explain its repeated-sampling interpretation. Identify why a small standard error alone does not establish that the sampling model is appropriate.

Use simulation to inspect the assumptions

A simulation can repeat a fully specified random experiment and record each sample mean or confidence interval. The resulting collection makes sampling variability visible. The fraction of simulated intervals covering the known generating mean estimates the coverage of that interval procedure under the simulation model. Simulation checks the implemented model; agreement does not prove the model describes real data. Compare independent samples with a deliberately dependent sampling process to see an assumption fail.

Teaching task: simulate the stated normal model, measure interval coverage, and explain what that experiment establishes and what remains untested.
